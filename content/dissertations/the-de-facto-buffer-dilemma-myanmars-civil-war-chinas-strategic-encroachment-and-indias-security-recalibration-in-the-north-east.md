---
title: "The De Facto Buffer Dilemma: Myanmar's Civil War, China's Strategic
  Encroachment, and India's Security Recalibration in the North-East"
author: Anand Kumar
institution: Pondicherry University
degree: M.A (Department of Politics & International Studies)
year: II
abstract: >
  When Myanmar's military seized power in February 2021, the consequences
  extended well beyond Naypyidaw. For India, the coup unravelled a carefully
  maintained geopolitical arrangement. In this arrangement, Myanmar had long
  served as a stabilising buffer along a 1,643-km frontier separating India's
  North-East from the wider arc of Chinese strategic influence. Four years into
  civil war, that buffer has collapsed in all but name. State authority has
  fragmented. Armed factions have multiplied. External involvement has deepened.
  Together, these developments have converted a manageable borderland into a
  zone of compounding strategic uncertainty.

  This paper investigates how Myanmar's unresolved internal conflict is forcing a fundamental recalibration of India's security posture in the North-East. Three interlocking dynamics guide the analysis. First, the breakdown of border governance has reinvigorated cross-border insurgent networks. It has also triggered significant refugee displacement into Manipur and Mizoram. This movement is straining both local administration and existing counter-insurgency frameworks. Second, China's expanding footprint is reshaping the regional balance of power. This expansion occurs through the China–Myanmar Economic Corridor, infrastructure financing, and reported contacts with select resistance groups. These developments increasingly work to India’s disadvantage. Third, New Delhi’s responses reveal a state navigating competing strategic pressures. These responses include the suspension of the Free Movement Regime, proposals for border fencing, and renewed investment in connectivity projects such as the Kaladan Multi-Modal Transit Transport Project. Together, they reflect an attempt to balance hard security imperatives with broader regional engagement ambitions.

  Drawing on qualitative geopolitical and policy analysis, this study engages theoretical debates on buffer state erosion, borderland security, and great-power rivalry. It situates India’s current predicament within wider structural shifts in Asian geopolitics. The paper concludes that India’s North-Eastern frontier has moved from a strategic periphery to a critical geopolitical interface. This transformation demands coherent, long-horizon policy thinking rather than reactive border management.

  Keywords: India–Myanmar Relations; North-East India; Border Security; Buffer States; China–Myanmar Economic Corridor; Regional Geopolitics.

# 1. Introduction

For years, India treated Myanmar asa buffer. That assumption no longer holds. Since the 2021 coup, Myanmar hasfragmented badly. The military has lost control over large parts of thecountry’s borderlands, including areas along the Indian frontier. At the sametime, the conflict has produced mass displacement on a regional scale, withUNHCR reporting 3.58 million internally displaced persons and 5.15 millionforcibly displaced people across the wider Myanmar situation by August 2025.

This shift creates a sharpstrategic puzzle. Why does the collapse of Myanmar’s buffer role impose heaviercosts on India than on China? India faces the direct effects first. Theseinclude refugee movement, cross-border armed activity, illicit trafficking, andthe disruption of its eastern connectivity plans. China also faces risk, but ithas been better placed to hedge, protect key projects, and deal with multipleactors at once.

This paper asks a simple question.How has Myanmar’s civil war changed the country from a buffer into a contestedstrategic corridor, and what does that mean for India’s North-East? The mainargument is that Myanmar no longer works as a classical buffer state. It hasentered a condition I call “de facto buffer erosion.” In this condition, astate still exists on the map, but it no longer performs the strategic functionexpected of a buffer.

The argument follows a clearsequence. Civil war fragments territory. Fragmentation pushes insecurity acrossborders. That vacuum invites external penetration, especially by China. India then has to recalibrate from passive border management to active frontierstrategy.

The paper uses qualitativegeopolitical analysis. It draws on academic literature, policy debate, thinktank material, and humanitarian reporting. Its main contribution is conceptualas well as empirical. It introduces “de facto buffer erosion” as a way toexplain how buffer states break down in practice, even when they survive inlaw. It also shows that India’s North-East is no longer just a difficultperiphery. It is becoming a strategic interface where domestic security,borderland politics, and great-power rivalry now meet.

# 2. Literature Review

The first body of literature isbuffer state theory. Classical geopolitics treated buffers as territorialcushions between stronger powers. Mackinder (1904) and Spykman (1944) both sawgeography as central to strategic insulation. Later work in internationalsecurity shifted the focus from static maps to regional patterns of threat andinterdependence. Buzan and Wæver (2003) made that shift especially clear. Yeteven this more advanced literature often treats buffer states as fixed units.It says less about what happens when a buffer survives formally but failsfunctionally.

That gap matters here. Myanmarstill exists as a sovereign state. But the problem is not legal disappearance.It is loss of stabilising capacity. Traditional buffer theory does not explainthat well. It tends to assume that once a state occupies intermediary space, itretains geopolitical meaning. This paper argues the opposite. A buffer onlybuffers when it can control territory, regulate violence, and mediate access.

The second body of work deals withborderland security and insurgency. Scott (2009) showed that upland SoutheastAsia has long resisted full state control. Borderlands are not empty margins.They are politically thick spaces with their own histories, networks, and formsof authority. Donnan and Wilson (1999), Newman (2006), Kolossov (2005), and vanSchendel (2005) all stress that borders are socially produced and unevenlygoverned. This literature helps explain why frontier zones do not respondneatly to state policy.

Work on insurgency adds anotherlayer. Staniland (2014) shows that armed groups survive through networks, localalliances, and fragmented authority. That is useful for understanding theIndia–Myanmar frontier. Still, much of this literature stops at the borderlanditself. It does not fully explain how civil war in one state can alter thesecurity doctrine of a neighbouring state. Spillovers are acknowledged, buttheir geopolitical consequences remain underdeveloped.

The third literature concernsChina’s peripheral strategy. Scholars of the Belt and Road Initiative andstrategic infrastructure show that roads, ports, and corridors are never onlyeconomic. They also create leverage, access, and political influence (Rolland,2017; Hillman, 2020). Studies on Myanmar’s border economies show that Chineseinfluence often moves through hybrid arrangements involving armed groups,traders, local elites, and state actors (Woods, 2011; Meehan, 2015). This isimportant because Myanmar is one of the clearest cases where infrastructure anddisorder coexist.

Recent evidence sharpens the point.The China–Myanmar Economic Corridor has remained central to Beijing’sinterests, and Crisis Group describes it as a roughly $20 billion project andChina’s only feasible overland route to the Indian Ocean. Yet much of theliterature still treats infrastructure as a bilateral state project. It saysless about how such corridors operate in the middle of civil war andterritorial fragmentation.

The fourth literature looks atIndia’s North-East. Here the central themes are insurgency, integration, anddevelopment. Baruah (2005, 2020) remains essential for understanding theregion’s deep political fractures. Other studies link Myanmar to India’s LookEast and Act East strategies, especially through transport and securitycooperation (Haacke, 2006; Myint-U, 2011; Paliwal, 2024). But this literatureoften assumes a minimally functioning Myanmar state on the other side of thefrontier. That assumption is now weak.

The wider gap is clear. Existingwork does not explain how a buffer state changes under the joint pressure ofcivil war and great-power competition. It also does not show clearly how thatchange restructures the security thinking of a neighbouring power. This paperaddresses that gap by treating the buffer not as a static object, but as adynamic strategic process.

# 3. Theoretical Framework

This paper proposes a “De FactoBuffer Erosion Framework.” The idea is simple. Buffer states are not definedonly by location. They are defined by what they do. They matter when theyabsorb pressure, regulate access, and reduce direct rivalry. When they stopdoing those things, buffer erosion begins.

The first dimension is territorialfragmentation. A buffer cannot function if the central state no longercontrols key borderlands. Once coercive authority is split among the army,militias, ethnic armed organisations, and resistance forces, the state losesits ability to stabilise strategic space. In Myanmar, this is not a temporarylocal problem. It has become a structural feature of the post-coup order.

The second dimension is securityexternalisation. Internal conflict does not stay inside national borderswhen terrain is porous and communities are linked across frontiers. Violencethen produces spillovers. These take the form of refugee movement, militantmobility, trafficking, and crisis diffusion. In such cases, the buffer becomesa transmitter of insecurity rather than a shield against it.

The third dimension is strategicpenetration. Once the buffer weakens, outside powers gain room to move.Mearsheimer’s offensive realism is useful here. Major powers exploit openingswhen the costs are low and the gains are durable (Mearsheimer, 2001). InMyanmar, this does not mean direct conquest. It means influence throughinfrastructure, mediation, political access, and selective ties with differentarmed actors.

The fourth dimension is peripheralrecalibration. This refers to the response of the neighbouring state mostexposed to the breakdown. In India’s case, the old model relied heavily onMyanmar as a territorial cushion. The new model requires direct management ofthe frontier. That includes border fencing, mobility control, selectivediplomacy, and the protection of strategic projects.

This framework speaks to RegionalSecurity Complex Theory. Buzan and Wæver (2003) argue that insecurity travelsmost intensely across regional proximity. Myanmar fits that logic. Its civilwar is not only domestic. It is reordering the strategic environment of theregion around it. The framework also draws on frontier governance theory.Borderlands are rarely governed in a clean, Weberian manner. They are governedthrough layered authority, negotiated violence, and informal arrangements.

The central theoretical claimfollows from this. Buffer states are processual, not static. Their geopoliticalrole depends on effective performance. Civil war can therefore change a bufferwithout erasing it. That is what makes de facto buffer erosion a usefulconcept. It captures the point where legal sovereignty remains, but strategicfunction collapses.

# 4. Methodology

This paper uses qualitativegeopolitical analysis. The goal is not to count every event. The goal is toexplain a strategic transformation. That requires close attention to sequence,meaning, and changing patterns of control.

The analysis relies onsecondary-source triangulation. It combines academic scholarship with policymaterial, humanitarian reporting, and strategic assessments. The sourcesinclude peer-reviewed books and journal articles, along with reports frominstitutions such as the International Crisis Group, IISS, SIPRI, and UNHCR.This mix is necessary because Myanmar’s conflict is fast-moving and unevenlydocumented.

A qualitative method is appropriatefor three reasons. First, the paper deals with a relational concept. Buffererosion is about changing function, not a single measurable variable. Second,the Myanmar conflict is marked by opaque reporting and contested claims aboutterritorial control. Third, the paper asks how India’s security thinking ischanging. That question is doctrinal and political as much as empirical.

There are clear limitations.Battlefield information from Myanmar is incomplete. Claims about control areoften politicised. Cross-border trafficking and insurgent movement are hard tomeasure with precision. To reduce these problems, the paper does not rely onany single source or any single indicator. It focuses instead on broad patternsthat appear across different kinds of material.

# 5. Myanmar Civil War and the Collapse of Buffer Function

Myanmar’s buffer role has weakenedbecause the state no longer controls enough of its own periphery. Since the2021 coup, the Tatmadaw has faced widening resistance from ethnic armedorganisations, local militias, and anti-regime forces. Crisis Group notes thatthe military has lost its hold on most of Myanmar’s borders, including the onewith India. By 2024, important stretches of the India-facing frontier were nolonger under firm central control.

This matters because a buffer isnot just a strip of land between two powers. It is a political mechanism. Itworks only when the state in the middle can police access, regulate armedactivity, and maintain enough order to prevent spillovers. Myanmar can nolonger do that consistently along its western and northwestern frontier.

The problem is especially seriousin Chin and Rakhine. These areas matter directly for India. They affect refugeeflows, insurgent movement, and the fate of projects like Kaladan. Once thoseregions become contested spaces, Myanmar stops functioning as a stabilisingintermediary.

This is why the phrase “buffercollapse” is more useful than “state collapse.” Myanmar has not disappeared. Itstill has a seat in international diplomacy. But its strategic role haschanged. It no longer reliably absorbs friction between India and China. It nowchannels disorder outward and invites competition inward.

The humanitarian picture reinforcesthis point. UNHCR’s 2025 figures show that displacement has reached a scaleconsistent with prolonged systemic conflict, not short-term unrest. A stateunder that degree of internal pressure cannot perform the classic work of abuffer.

# 6. Cross-Border Security Spillovers into India

The first effect of this breakdownis spillover into India’s North-East. The India–Myanmar border has become moreporous as conflict deepens on the Myanmar side. Indian debate on the issueincreasingly links border vulnerability to insurgent movement, arms flows, drugtrafficking, and unmanaged mobility. This is one reason why the border nowfigures more centrally in India’s internal security discourse.

Refugee movement is the mostvisible spillover. One 2025 estimate put the number of people who had enteredIndia from Myanmar since the coup at 78,731, including over 34,000 in Mizoram.UNHCR also reported that fresh fighting in Chin State in late June 2025 drovemore than 6,500 people into Champhai district in Mizoram before some laterreturned. These movements are not random. They track the rhythm of violenceacross the frontier.

The second spillover is militantopportunity. The India–Myanmar border has long been used by insurgent groupsfor shelter, transit, and exchange. That pattern becomes easier when stateauthority collapses on one side. Recent analysis continues to connect porousborder conditions with insurgent risks and cross-border armed activityaffecting India’s North-East. Even when the scale of revival varies, theenabling structure is real.

The third spillover is illicitcirculation. Conflict weakens oversight. That creates room for smuggling,informal taxation, and black-market trade in arms and narcotics. These are notmarginal issues. They alter local political economies and complicate civilianadministration on the Indian side as well.

The larger point is simple. India’seastern frontier is no longer only a border. It is part of a conflict system.That changes how security has to be understood and managed.

# 7. China’s Strategic Encroachment

China has adjusted to Myanmar’sfragmentation with greater flexibility than India. Beijing does not need fullorder everywhere. It needs leverage over key routes, projects, and actors. Thatgives it an advantage in a fractured setting.

This is clear in the case of theChina–Myanmar Economic Corridor. Crisis Group describes CMEC as a roughly $20billion project and China’s only feasible overland route to the Indian Ocean.That makes Myanmar strategically valuable well beyond its domestic politics.Even when the country is unstable, the corridor remains central to Beijing’swestern access strategy.

China’s approach has beenpragmatic. It has kept ties with the junta but has also adjusted to changingrealities on the ground. Where useful, it has tolerated pressure from armedgroups. Where necessary, it has backed the military more directly to avoid completedisorder. This is not confusion. It is hedging.

Infrastructure deepens the effect.Ports, roads, pipelines, and logistics corridors generate political influencewhen the host state is weak. In such settings, the external power that can talkto multiple local actors gains strategic depth. That is what China hasincreasingly done in Myanmar.

For India, this creates a doubleloss. India loses the old insulating function that Myanmar once offered. China,meanwhile, gains a larger operating space in the same theatre. The issue is nolonger simply Chinese presence in Myanmar. It is Chinese penetration into avacuum created by buffer erosion.

# 8. India’s Security Recalibration

India has not stood still. Itspolicy has begun to change. But the change is uneven and still incomplete. Onesign of this is diplomatic adaptation. Crisis Group reports that India haswidened contact with anti-regime forces controlling parts of the border whilestill maintaining ties with Naypyitaw. That marks a clear shift from olderpolicy habits.

Another sign is harder frontiermanagement. India has moved to tighten movement controls and expand borderfencing. By August 2025, about 401 kilometres of the 1,643-kilometreIndia–Myanmar border had been fenced. This reflects a wider move away from passivedependence on Myanmar’s internal stability.

The end of the Free Movement Regimealso fits this shift. Reporting on the issue shows that New Delhi increasinglylinked the regime to security concerns such as smuggling, insurgent movement,and porous-border risks. In policy terms, the border is being reimagined lessas a social corridor and more as a strategic fault line.

Yet India cannot rely on closurealone. Its eastern connectivity goals still depend on access through Myanmar.The Kaladan project, trade ambitions, and wider Act East thinking all requireworkable relations in territories that are no longer fully controlled by theMyanmar state. This means India must combine security tightening with politicalflexibility.

That is the core of recalibration.India is moving from passive buffer reliance to active frontier management. Butit still has to decide what kind of frontier state it wants to be.

# 9. From Buffer to Interface: Reframing the North-East

This shift changes how theNorth-East itself should be understood. For a long time, the region was framedmainly through insurgency, underdevelopment, and internal integration. Thatframe is no longer enough.

The North-East is now betterunderstood as a strategic interface. It is where domestic governance,borderland instability, regional connectivity, and India–China competitionmeet. That does not erase internal problems. But it changes their meaning.Internal security and external geopolitics now overlap much more directly thanbefore.

Under the old view, the region wasa periphery. Under the new view, it is a hinge. That is a major analyticalchange. It also has policy consequences. A hinge region cannot be governed withthe same mindset used for a distant border margin.

# 10. Tensions, Trade-offs, and Strategic Constraints

India’s new position brings hardtrade-offs. The first is between engagement and containment. India needsrelations with the Myanmar military, but it also needs practical channels tonon-state actors who control parts of the frontier. Ignoring either side wouldreduce Indian leverage.

The second trade-off is betweendevelopment and securitisation. Stronger controls may reduce short-term risk.But they can also disrupt border livelihoods and weaken the local foundationsof connectivity. A sealed frontier may be safer in one sense and weaker inanother.

The third trade-off is betweenbalancing China and avoiding escalation. India cannot simply retreat and leavethe field open. But it also cannot turn Myanmar into a direct proxy contestwithout raising larger regional risks. That calls for restraint as well asresolve.

These tensions explain why India’sresponse looks mixed. It is not only policy hesitation. It is also the productof a genuinely difficult strategic environment.

# 11. Comparative Perspective

Myanmar is not the only case wherea buffer has broken down. Afghanistan showed how the erosion of intermediaryorder can export instability across borders. Ukraine shows how buffercontestation can become the very site of direct geopolitical struggle. The twocases are very different, but both show that buffers are unstable arrangements,not permanent facts.

Myanmar adds an importantvariation. Here the buffer is not collapsing because of formal annexation orinterstate war alone. It is being hollowed out by civil war, fragmentedauthority, and external competition at the same time. That makes it a useful casefor broader theory.

The comparative lesson is clear. Abuffer may survive on the map and fail in practice. That is exactly what theidea of de facto buffer erosion is meant to capture.

# 12. Policy Implications and Strategic Roadmap

India needs a more integratedfrontier strategy. Border management, refugee handling, intelligencecoordination, and connectivity planning can no longer be treated as separatefiles. They are now parts of one strategic problem.

First, India should develop aborder doctrine tailored to different sectors of the frontier. Some stretchesrequire tighter control. Others require regulated mobility and strong localengagement. A single model will not work across the entire border.

Second, India should deepenmulti-level diplomacy. Exclusive reliance on Naypyitaw is no longer realistic.Stable access now depends on working relations with local authorities and armedactors in border regions, even if those ties remain informal and tightlymanaged.

Third, connectivity and securityshould be planned together. Projects like Kaladan should not be treated aspurely economic assets. They are strategic corridors. That means routeprotection, local legitimacy, and political negotiation are just as importantas physical construction.

Fourth, India needs a more groundedChina strategy in Myanmar. Matching China rupee for yuan is unlikely. But Indiacan compete through political credibility, regional partnerships, and betteralignment between local needs and national strategy. In fragmented borderlands,legitimacy often matters as much as scale.

# 13. Conclusion

Myanmar no longer functions as aclassical buffer for India. Civil war has changed its strategic role. It hasbecome a contested corridor shaped by territorial fragmentation, cross-borderspillovers, Chinese penetration, and Indian adjustment.

The concept of de facto buffererosion helps explain this shift. It shows that a buffer does not fail onlywhen a state disappears. It can also fail when the state remains but loses theability to stabilise the space around it. That is what has happened in Myanmar.

This matters for both theory andpolicy. Theoretically, it pushes buffer state analysis beyond static geography.Empirically, it shows why India’s North-East can no longer be seen only throughthe lens of domestic insurgency. It is now a strategic interface in Asiangeopolitics. India’s security doctrine will have to adapt to that fact.
