# Abstract

Africa's digital push has movedwell past the question of connectivity. What is at stake now is control—oversystems, over data, and over the everyday rules that govern digital life. Asgovernments try to expand digital services with limited resources, the choiceof external partners is no longer neutral. It carries political and strategicconsequences. So, the issue is not simply whether India can compete with Chinain scale. The more interesting question is whether it is offering somethingfundamentally different. 

This paper uses the idea of DigitalPublic Infrastructure (DPI) diplomacy to make sense of India's approach. Itrefers to the outward extension of systems built on openness andinteroperability—identity layers, payment rails, service platforms that do notbind countries too tightly to a single vendor. The distinction matters. Unlikeinfrastructure-heavy models, this approach works through design choices thatquietly shape how systems function over time. 

The analysis compares India'sengagement with China's Digital Silk Road across selected African contexts. Itdraws on policy material, development reports, and a few grounded examples.Rather than treating both models as interchangeable, it looks at how theydiffer in governance, data handling, costs, and longer-term implications. 

India's experience with India Stackand UPI does suggest a viable pathway—more flexible, often cheaper, andsomewhat more attentive to sovereignty concerns. Still, the constraints arereal. Financing is thinner. Institutional presence is uneven. Chinese firms, bycontrast, are already deeply embedded. 

Africa, in all this, is not just astage for rivalry. It shapes outcomes. Choices made here are likely to producehybrid arrangements and those, in turn, will influence how the broader digitalorder evolves.

# 1. Introduction

Digital infrastructure has ceasedto be a purely technical matter of pipes, protocols, and platforms; it hasbecome a domain in which states negotiate authority, extract rents, and projectinfluence beyond their borders. Where earlier debates about Africa's digitalfuture centred on connectivity—how many people had access to mobile networks orbroadband—the more consequential questions today concern governance: who setsthe standards that identity systems and payment rails run on, where dataphysically and legally resides, and how tightly a recipient state becomes boundto a single external vendor (Erie & Streinz, 2021; Couture & Toupin,2019). This shift from connectivity to governance has coincided with theemergence of two partly overlapping, partly divergent external offers toAfrican states: China's Digital Silk Road (DSR), an extension of the Belt andRoad Initiative combining state finance, telecommunications hardware, cloudinfrastructure, and smart-city systems (Hillman, 2021), and India's outward promotionof Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI)—modular, interoperable identity,payment, and data-exchange layers modelled on India Stack and the UnifiedPayments Interface (UPI).

The puzzle this paper addresses isnot whether India can match China's scale of investment; on that metric, thecomparison is settled before it begins. The more analytically productivequestion is whether India's DPI-centred engagement constitutes a *qualitativelydistinct* model of digital partnership. One organised around openness andreduced vendor lock-in—or whether it merely supplies rhetorical alternativebranding to what remains, in practice, a complementary adjunct to Chinese-builtinfrastructure. Existing scholarship has extensively theorised China's digitalexpansion as a form of technological statecraft and transnational datagovernance (Erie & Streinz, 2021; Hillman, 2021), and a parallel body ofwork examines digital sovereignty and dependency in African contexts (Jili,2026). What remains underdeveloped is a systematic account of India's outwardDPI promotion. As a form of diplomacy in its own right, distinct fromconventional digital aid, e-governance assistance, or infrastructure diplomacy.This is the gap this paper addresses.

The central research question is:how and to what extent does India's DPI diplomacy differ from China's DigitalSilk Road in institutional design, governance logic, data-sovereigntyimplications, financing model, and developmental consequences for Africanstates? The paper's thesis is that India's DPI diplomacy represents apotentially distinctive model. It foregrounds interoperability, modularity, andreduced vendor lock-in. However, its capacity to function as a full alternativeremains conditional. It depends on African institutional capacity, financingsustainability, data-governance safeguards, and the strategic choices thatAfrican states make when combining external offers. Accordingly, the mostprobable outcome is not the binary substitution of one external model foranother. Instead, it is the emergence of plural, hybrid, and country-specificdigital orders.

Methodologically, the paper relieson qualitative comparative institutional analysis, interpretive reading ofpolicy documents and development reports, and a small number of illustrativeAfrican cases used for theory-building rather than causal generalisation. Theremainder of the paper proceeds as follows: it reviews the literature ondigital geopolitics, infrastructure power, and digital sovereignty; develops anoriginal analytical framework for DPI diplomacy; specifies methodology and caselogic; examines digital infrastructure as strategic power in Africa; analysesIndia's and China's respective offers; foregrounds African agency andhybridity; compares the two models directly; proposes a policy framework; andconcludes by situating the argument within debates on South–South cooperationand multipolar digital order.

# 2. Literature Review: Digital Geopolitics, Infrastructure, and Sovereignty

Scholarship on digital geopoliticshas largely converged on two dominant framings. The first treats digitalinfrastructure as a direct instrument of great-power competition, in whichstates deploy technology exports, standards diplomacy, and infrastructurefinance to expand spheres of influence. Erie and Streinz's (2021) concept ofthe "Beijing Effect" exemplifies this approach. They theorise thatChina's Digital Silk Road combines push factors, such as state-backed financingand bundled infrastructure packages, with pull factors, such as recipientstates' demand for rapid digitalisation. This combination extends Chineseinfluence over transnational data governance. It does so without requiringformal legal harmonisation. Hillman (2021) extends this analysis empirically.He documents how Chinese firms, including Huawei, ZTE, and associated cloud andfibre-optic providers, have become embedded across Asian, African, and LatinAmerican digital ecosystems. He argues that infrastructure ownership confersdurable leverage over technical standards and data flows. This literatureusefully identifies infrastructure as a strategic asset, but it risksflattening recipient states into passive terrain upon which great-power rivalryunfolds.

A second, related body of worktreats digital infrastructure as ostensibly neutral development infrastructure,evaluated primarily on efficiency, cost, and service-delivery outcomes, withinsufficient attention to how design choices encode geopolitical andinstitutional consequences. Studies on Africa's digital transformation drawingon World Bank and African Union material frequently adopt this technocraticregister (World Bank, 2024; African Union, 2020), cataloguing gaps inconnectivity, regulatory capacity, and fiscal frameworks without theorising howthe choice of external partner reshapes long-term state–citizen andstate–vendor relations.

The concept of infrastructure powersupplies a corrective. Larkin's (2013) anthropological account ofinfrastructure as simultaneously material, aesthetic, and politicaldemonstrates that infrastructures are never simply neutral conduits; theyencode temporal promises, distribute access unevenly, and constitute the verypossibility of connection or exclusion. This insight has been extended intodigital sovereignty debates. Couture and Toupin (2019) show that "digitalsovereignty" is a polysemous and contested concept. It is invoked bystates, corporations, and civil society for different purposes. These rangefrom data localisation mandates to techno-nationalist industrial policy. Theycaution against treating claims of digital sovereignty as self-evidentlyemancipatory. Jili's (2026) ethnographic study of technical educationinitiatives in Kenya deepens this caution considerably. The study argues thatAfrican digital sovereignty projects often operate as performances of"stateness." These projects frequently reinforce dependency onChinese and Western corporate partners. This occurs even though they arediscursively framed as efforts to build autonomy. This finding is critical forthe present paper: it warns against treating any externally sourced digitalmodel—including India's—as inherently emancipatory simply because it is framedaround openness.

Postcolonial anddependency-theoretic scholarship supplies further ballast. Mbembe's (2001)analysis of power in the postcolony and Ferguson's (2006) account of Africa'sintegration into neoliberal global capital circuits support this perspective.They suggest that digital dependency is best understood as a continuation ofolder asymmetries. These asymmetries include inequalities in capital,expertise, and institutional capacity. Rather than being a wholly newphenomenon, digital dependency reflects the persistence of these structuralimbalances. Gagliardone's (2016) study of Ethiopia's state-led technologypolitics similarly demonstrates that African governments actively shape, ratherthan merely absorb, the terms of technological engagement—selecting vendors,negotiating financing terms, and reworking imported systems to serve domesticpolitical projects. Nyabola's (2018) account of Kenya's digital politicsreinforces this emphasis on domestic agency, showing how local politicaldynamics, not external design alone, determine platform outcomes.

Parallel debates on platform powerand standards-setting sharpen the analytical stakes. Farrell and Newman's(2019) concept of "weaponised interdependence" demonstrates howcontrol over network chokepoints confers coercive leverage independent offormal territorial control. These chokepoints include financial messagingsystems, cloud infrastructure, and payment rails. This framework is directlyrelevant to assessing vendor lock-in risks in both the Chinese and Indianmodels. Kwet's (2019) treatment of digital colonialism extends dependencytheory into the platform era. He argues that ownership of the technicalinfrastructural "stack," rather than merely ownership of data,constitutes the decisive locus of structural power. This claim has direct relevancefor evaluating both the Digital Silk Road (DSR) and Digital PublicInfrastructure (DPI). Taylor's (2017) framework of data justice, meanwhile,insists that data governance questions cannot be separated from broaderquestions of structural inequality and rights, cautioning against purelytechnical readings of interoperability.

Literature on South–Southcooperation and development diplomacy offers a final, underused resource. Mawdsley(2012) and Chaturvedi, Fues, and Sidiropoulos (2012) document how emergingpowers, including India, frame development assistance in horizontal andnon-conditional terms. They argue that this approach differs rhetorically fromtraditional Western aid. However, the practical distinctiveness of these claimsremains contested. This literature has been applied extensively to Chineseengagement but only thinly to India's digital diplomacy specifically.

Taken together, this literaturedemonstrates a clear gap. Scholarship on China's Digital Silk Road istheoretically rich, treating infrastructure explicitly as an instrument ofstatecraft (Erie & Streinz, 2021; Hillman, 2021). Scholarship on digital sovereigntyand dependency in Africa is similarly sophisticated but tends to treat allexternally sourced digital systems with structurally similar suspicion, withoutdifferentiating design logics (Jili, 2026; Couture & Toupin, 2019). What ismissing is a framework that (a) takes seriously the possibility that designchoices—modularity, interoperability, open standards—produce materiallydifferent governance and dependency effects than vendor-bundled, state-financedinfrastructure packages, while (b) refusing to treat any model, includingIndia's, as automatically democratic or emancipatory. This paper's concept ofDigital Public Infrastructure Diplomacy is developed to fill precisely thisgap.

# 3. Theoretical Framework: Digital Public Infrastructure Diplomacy

This paper defines Digital PublicInfrastructure (DPI) Diplomacy as the strategic externalisation, by a state, ofinteroperable, modular, and publicly oriented digital systems. It also includesthe externalisation of standards, technical expertise, governance models, andcapacity-building arrangements. These efforts are undertaken to shapedevelopment partnerships, institutional preferences, and the internationaldigital order. This definition deliberately foregrounds design logic—openness,modularity, interoperability—rather than the identity of the exporting state orthe scale of financial commitment, distinguishing it from adjacent categoriesof engagement.

DPI diplomacy differs from digitalaid, which typically involves grant-based technology transfer without embeddedarchitectural commitments to openness. It differs from e-governancecooperation, which centres on discrete administrative digitisation projectsrather than exportable infrastructural layers. It differs from traditionalinfrastructure diplomacy, exemplified by the Digital Silk Road, in whichstate-backed finance and vendor-specific hardware bundle infrastructureownership with long-term maintenance dependency (Erie & Streinz, 2021;Hillman, 2021). It differs from cyber diplomacy, which concerns norms of statebehaviour in cyberspace rather than the architecture of civilianservice-delivery systems. And it differs from generic technology exports, whichinvolve commercial sales without an accompanying governance philosophy orientedtoward public-interest use.

The framework operationalises DPIdiplomacy along five analytical dimensions, each posed as a diagnostic questionrather than a normative verdict:

Dimension

Analytical Question

Institutional architecture

Is the system open,  interoperable, modular, and adaptable to domestic law?

Financing and ownership

Who finances, owns, maintains,  and upgrades the infrastructure?

Data sovereignty

Where does data reside, who  accesses it, and what legal safeguards govern its use?

Vendor dependence

Does the arrangement reduce or  deepen technological lock-in?

Local capacity and agency

Can domestic institutions adapt,  regulate, maintain, and reshape the system?

 

These dimensions draw directly onthe infrastructure-power literature (Larkin, 2013), onweaponised-interdependence theory's attention to chokepoints and leverage(Farrell & Newman, 2019), and on digital-sovereignty scholarship'sinsistence that sovereignty is enacted through practice rather than declaredthrough rhetoric (Couture & Toupin, 2019; Jili, 2026). Crucially, theframework does not assume that openness automatically translates intorecipient-state autonomy. Jili's (2026) findings on Kenya demonstrate thatcapacity-building initiatives framed around sovereignty can reinscribedependency when local institutions lack the technical and fiscal capacity tosustain systems independently. Applied to India's model, this means modularityis a necessary but not sufficient condition for genuine sovereigntyenhancement; it must be accompanied by financing sustainability and localinstitutional capacity, the very variables in which India's offer iscomparatively weaker than China's.

The framework's purpose is not torank India above China. Instead, it provides a comparative instrument forassessing whether a given digital partnership expands or restrictsrecipient-state autonomy. This assessment is made regardless of the exporting country'srhetorical self-presentation.

# 4. Methodology and Case Logic

This paper employs qualitativeinstitutional analysis combined with comparative foreign-policy and regulatoryanalysis. It interprets primary policy documents. These include the Report ofIndia's G20 Task Force on Digital Public Infrastructure (Government of India,Ministry of Finance, 2024, as reported in Press Information Bureau, 2024), UNDP'sDPI Playbook and SDG Compendium materials issued during India's G20 presidency(UNDP, 2023), and the African Union's Digital Transformation Strategy forAfrica 2020–2030 (African Union, 2020). The paper also draws on institutionalreports (World Bank, 2024) and scholarly literature on Chinese digitalexpansion (Hillman, 2021; Erie & Streinz, 2021) and African digitalgovernance (Jili, 2026).

The paper compares India'sDPI-oriented engagement with China's Digital Silk Road engagement throughselected illustrative African contexts. It draws, where credible evidence isavailable, on cases from Kenya, Ethiopia, and other countries discussed in theliterature. These cases are used for theory-building rather than forstatistical or causal generalisation across all fifty-four African states. Thecomparative criteria applied throughout are: infrastructure design, financingstructure, governance model, data and cybersecurity implications, role ofprivate firms, institutional adaptability, and long-term sovereignty effects. Thisstructured comparison, rather than a single-country deep case study, allows theanalysis to test the five dimensions of the DPI diplomacy frameworksystematically against both external models. At the same time, it remainsattentive to the diversity of African institutional capacities. A single-casedesign would obscure this heterogeneity.

# 5. Digital Infrastructure as Strategic Power in Africa

African states' demand for digitalidentity systems, payment platforms, e-governance architecture, cloudinfrastructure, and telecommunications networks has expanded rapidly. Thisgrowth is driven by financial inclusion goals, service-delivery pressures, andcontinental integration ambitions under the African Union's DigitalTransformation Strategy. The strategy envisions a single digital market andharmonised data-governance frameworks by 2030 (African Union, 2020). Africanstates' demand, however, is met overwhelmingly through external supply. MostAfrican states possess limited domestic capacity to design, finance, andmaintain large-scale digital systems independently. This structural conditionis documented across the World Bank's technical assessments of digital-marketregulation on the continent (World Bank, 2024).

This dependence carries strategicimplications beyond service efficiency. Infrastructure choices determine whichactors control identity verification, transaction clearing, and data storage.As a result, they shape the relationship between citizens, the state, andexternal technology providers (Larkin, 2013). Where digital identity or paymentsystems are built on proprietary, vendor-specific architecture, switching costsincrease. As a result, the state's future regulatory latitude becomesconstrained by the technical choices embedded at the system-design stage. Theseconstraints affect areas such as data localisation, interoperability mandates,and competition policy (Farrell & Newman, 2019). Where surveillance-capableinfrastructure such as smart-city or public-safety systems is layered onto coreconnectivity, additional risks to civil liberties and political accountabilityemerge. Risks that scholarship on Chinese technology exports has documentedwithout treating them as uniformly deterministic (Hillman, 2021).

It is essential, however, to avoidtreating "Africa" as a uniform bloc facing these pressuresidentically. State capacity varies enormously: Rwanda and Kenya have developedcomparatively sophisticated digital-economy strategies and regulatory institutions,while other states lack the procurement expertise or regulatory autonomy tonegotiate favourable terms with any external partner (Jili, 2026; Gagliardone,2016). Negotiating leverage also varies with fiscal position, debt exposure,and regional-bloc coordination capacity. The African Union's continentaldigital-integration agenda represents an attempt to aggregate bargaining powerand harmonise standards across this heterogeneity, but its implementationremains uneven across member states (African Union, 2020). This variation iscentral to the argument developed later in this paper. The outcome ofIndia–China digital competition in Africa is not determined solely by thedesign logic of either external offer. Instead, it is co-produced by thediffering capacities of African institutions to evaluate, negotiate, and adaptthe digital solutions they receive.

# 6. India's DPI Diplomacy: Promise, Design, and Constraints

India's outward DPI promotion restson the domestic architecture of India Stack. India Stack is a layered set ofopen-API identity, data-exchange, and payment protocols. It also builds on the UnifiedPayments Interface (UPI). UPI enables real-time, interoperable transactionsacross banks and non-bank payment providers. It does so without locking usersinto the proprietary system of a single financial institution. This modulardesign logic was consolidated into a formal international policy agenda duringIndia's 2023 G20 presidency. The presidency produced a G20-endorsed commondescription of DPI as secure and interoperable digital systems built on openstandards. It identified governance and inclusion as core components. It alsoproduced a DPI Playbook and SDG Compendium, jointly coordinated with the UNDP(UNDP, 2023). The subsequent Report of India's G20 Task Force on Digital PublicInfrastructure (Press Information Bureau, 2024) extended this agenda. Itexplicitly framed DPI as a transferable model for the Global South. It alsopositioned India as a knowledge partner rather than merely an infrastructurevendor.

The design-based advantages thismodel offers are analytically distinguishable from those of vendor-bundledinfrastructure. Because India Stack's protocols are open and interoperable,adapting countries retain latitude to build local applications atop the samerails without committing to a single foreign vendor's proprietary stack. Inprinciple reducing the vendor-lock-in risk that scholarship on weaponisedinterdependence identifies as a core source of asymmetric leverage (Farrell& Newman, 2019). The modular architecture also permits phased, lower-costadoption. Governments can implement payment interoperability or digitalidentity layers incrementally rather than purchasing an entire bundledinfrastructure package. A feature consistent with the G20 Task Force's emphasison "minimum viable" DPI implementation pathways (Press InformationBureau, 2024). This lower capital threshold is potentially significant forAfrican states with constrained fiscal space, and it aligns rhetorically withthe DPI framework's professed emphasis on public governance over proprietarycontrol.

These advantages, however, areconditional and should not be conflated with automatic gains in democraticaccountability or data protection. India's own domestic experience with Aadhaarhas generated sustained controversy. The concerns include data-protectionsafeguards, exclusion risks for citizens who are unable to complete biometricauthentication, and the extent of private-sector access to identity data. Theseissues mirror the very sovereignty risks that the DPI framework claims tomitigate (Couture & Toupin, 2019). Openness at the protocol level does not,by itself, guarantee that recipient states possess the regulatory capacity toenforce data-protection safeguards, cybersecurity standards, or algorithmicaccountability after a system is deployed. As Jili's (2026) Kenyan casedemonstrates, capacity-building programmes framed around sovereignty can stillcreate dependency on external technical expertise. This dependency may extendto system maintenance, upgrades, and cybersecurity monitoring. India's institutionaland financial presence in Africa also remains considerably thinner than China's.India lacks an equivalent to China's state-backed concessional lendingapparatus for digital infrastructure. Its diplomatic and technical-assistancefootprint includes bilateral lines of credit, ITEC training programmes, andselected e-governance and telemedicine partnerships. However, these initiativesare neither as systematically financed nor as deeply embedded across thecontinent as the commercial and infrastructural presence of Chinese firms (Erie& Streinz, 2021). The result is a model that is normatively attractive indesign terms but materially constrained in its delivery capacity. This tensionlies at the heart of the paper's thesis. It is central to evaluating theprospects of India's DPI diplomacy in Africa.

# 7. China's Digital Silk Road: Scale, Embeddedness, and Dependency Risks

China's Digital Silk Road combinesstate-directed financing with corporate expansion by firms such as Huawei andZTE. It also offers integrated delivery of telecommunications networks,fibre-optic and submarine cable infrastructure, cloud services, e-commerceplatforms, and smart-city systems. Together, these elements form a bundledpackage that few external actors currently match in scale (Hillman, 2021). Erieand Streinz (2021) theorise this expansion through the "BeijingEffect." They argue that China shapes transnational data governance lessthrough formal legal harmonisation than through the practical diffusion ofinfrastructure, technical standards, and operational norms embedded in thesystems it builds. In this model, influence is exercised through infrastructureownership rather than treaty-based rule-setting.

The advantages of this model forAfrican governments are substantial and should not be dismissed. Chinese firmsoffer integrated and bundled solutions. These packages combine hardware,financing, construction, and after-sales maintenance in a single offering. Theyalso provide rapid implementation and draw on extensive commercial experienceaccumulated across multiple African markets since the early 2000s (Hillman,2021). This bundling reduces transaction costs for governments that lack theprocurement capacity to coordinate multiple vendors. In specific cases, Chineseconcessional financing has also enabled the expansion of digital connectivitythat might otherwise have been fiscally unattainable (Jili, 2026).

The corresponding risks are welldocumented in the literature without requiring deterministic claims aboutinevitable dependency. Vendor lock-in is a structural feature of bundledinfrastructure provision. Once telecommunications backbone, data-centre, andcloud infrastructure are built on proprietary Chinese hardware and softwarestandards, switching costs for recipient states increase significantly.Long-term maintenance and upgrade cycles also often remain tied to the originalvendor (Erie & Streinz, 2021; Farrell & Newman, 2019). Debt exposureassociated with concessional infrastructure loans raises fiscal sovereigntyconcerns in some contexts. In addition, the surveillance capabilities embeddedin smart-city and public-safety systems exported alongside connectivityinfrastructure have attracted significant scrutiny. These concerns focus ontheir implications for data governance and civil liberties (Hillman, 2021). Jili's(2026) Kenyan fieldwork further shows that Chinese firms' presence extendsbeyond hardware. It also includes workforce training and capacity-buildingprogrammes. Although these programmes are presented as enhancing sovereignty,they can further entrench technical dependency on Chinese corporate expertiseat the operational level.

At the same time, deterministicreadings of Chinese digital influence overstate structural inevitability.Host-state institutions retain meaningful influence over procurement terms,regulatory frameworks, and competitive tendering. Kenya's own digital-economygovernance, for instance, reflects active negotiation with multiple externalpartners rather than passive absorption of a single external model(Gagliardone, 2016; Jili, 2026). The empirical picture, in other words, is oneof asymmetric but not unilateral influence. The depth of dependency created byChinese infrastructure ultimately depends on the regulatory choices made byAfrican states.

# 8. African Agency and Hybrid Digital Orders

The most significant corrective toa binary India-versus-China framing lies in recognising that African states arestrategic actors who select, combine, adapt, and at times resist externaldigital offers rather than passively receiving them. Empirically, Africandigital ecosystems already display extensive component mixing. Chinese-builtfibre and mobile-network hardware frequently coexist with Western cloudplatforms, such as Amazon Web Services and Microsoft Azure. They also operatealongside American-dominated mobile operating systems and social-mediaplatforms. Increasingly, they are complemented by interoperable paymentarchitectures inspired by, or directly modelled on, India's UPI (Jili, 2026). Thislayered coexistence, rather than exclusive alignment with a single externalpartner, constitutes what this paper terms hybrid digital sovereignty.Under this arrangement, sovereignty is exercised not through the completerejection of external dependency. Instead, it is achieved through thecalibrated diversification of multiple external suppliers across differentlayers of the digital stack.

Kenya illustrates this logicconcretely. Chinese firms have built the foundational connectivity layer,including cables, towers, and data-centre hardware. American firms dominate thesoftware and platform layer. Meanwhile, Kenyan regulatory and civil-societyactors continue to negotiate data governance, workforce training, and marketaccess with both sets of partners simultaneously (Jili, 2026). Ethiopia's morestate-controlled digital economy strategy presents a contrasting model. It hashistorically combined domestic political control over telecommunications withselective foreign technical partnerships. This reflects a differentconfiguration of the same underlying agency (Gagliardone, 2016). Theseillustrations are not intended to provide exhaustive country profiles. Instead,they demonstrate a broader logic. The degree and form of hybridity varyaccording to state capacity, procurement expertise, and regulatory autonomy.However, the underlying pattern of strategic diversification recurs acrosscontexts where institutions possess sufficient capacity to exercise it.

This has direct implications forhow India's DPI diplomacy should be evaluated. Rather than asking whether Indiacan displace China as Africa's primary digital partner, a more empiricallygrounded question is whether African states can incorporate India Stack-stylemodular protocols into their already hybrid digital-infrastructure environment.For example, they may layer interoperable payment or identity systems on top ofChinese-built connectivity infrastructure. This approach would allow them tocapture the sovereignty-enhancing benefits of modularity without requiring thewholesale replacement of existing Chinese investment. Where state capacity isstrong enough to negotiate and integrate multiple external offers deliberately,hybridity itself becomes a sovereignty-enhancing strategy; where capacity isweak, hybridity may instead reflect uncoordinated dependency accumulationacross multiple external patrons simultaneously (Jili, 2026).

This comparison demonstrates thatIndia is not currently a complete substitute for China in material or financialterms. # 10. Policy Framework: Building Sovereignty-Enhancing DPI Partnerships

Translating this analysis intopolicy requires recommendations addressed jointly to India, Africangovernments, regional institutions, and development partners, each linkeddirectly to the constraints and opportunities identified above.

First, DPI partnerships should beco-designed rather than exported wholesale. African technical agencies shouldparticipate in adapting India Stack-derived protocols to their domestic legaland administrative contexts from the outset. They should not simply adoptpre-built templates. This approach addresses the local-capacity dimensionidentified as the weakest link in the current DPI framework (Jili, 2026).Second, every DPI partnership should embed enforceable data-protection,cybersecurity, and human-rights safeguards at the point of system design, giventhat modularity alone does not guarantee sovereignty absent regulatoryenforcement capacity (Couture & Toupin, 2019). Third, financing andlifecycle-maintenance arrangements should be transparent and designed for thelong term rather than limited to individual projects. This would address thefinancing constraint that currently limits India's comparative offer relative toChina's concessional lending apparatus. Fourth, India and African partnersshould jointly support local technical-capacity building and domesticinnovation ecosystems. Not as a substitute for structural investment, as Jili's(2026) critique of capacity-building rhetoric warns, but as a complement togenuine transfer of system-ownership rights. Fifth, open standards should bepromoted while explicitly guarding against new forms of dependency on externaltechnical expertise for system maintenance and upgrades. Sixth, India andAfrican regional institutions should establish dedicated forums on DPIgovernance, interoperable payments, digital identity, and cross-border datapolicy. These forums could operate through the African Union's digitalintegration agenda. They would build on the multilateral consensus alreadyachieved through the G20 DPI framework (UNDP, 2023; African Union, 2020). Seventh,African governments should adopt explicit diversification strategies andpublic-interest procurement rules. These measures should treat hybridity as adeliberate strategy to strengthen sovereignty rather than as an unintendedoutcome of uncoordinated engagement with external partners. Finally,independent audit and accountability mechanisms should be established fordigital identity, payment, and public-service systems, regardless of theexternal partner involved. This would ensure that neither India's nor China'sdigital initiatives operate without effective domestic oversight.

# 11. Conclusion

This paper has argued that India'sDigital Public Infrastructure diplomacy constitutes a conceptually distinctmodel of digital partnership. It is organised around modularity,interoperability, and reduced vendor lock-in. It is therefore more than a rhetoricalvariant of existing digital-aid or infrastructure-diplomacy models. The DigitalPublic Infrastructure Diplomacy framework developed in this paper isoperationalised through five dimensions: institutional architecture, financingand ownership, data sovereignty, vendor dependence, and local capacity. Theframework demonstrates that design choices embedded in digital systems producelasting geopolitical, institutional, and sovereignty effects. These effectsarise independently of the exporting state's declared intentions.

This analysis shows that digitalinfrastructure must be understood as a site of institutional power andsovereignty negotiation rather than as a neutral development tool. It alsoargues that the international digital order is being shaped incrementally throughthe accumulation of specific infrastructural choices made across dozens ofAfrican jurisdictions. India's model is potentially significant because itoffers African states a genuine design-level alternative to bundled vendorlock-in. However, its effectiveness remains conditional. India's financingcapacity, institutional presence, and implementation support continue to lagbehind China's deeply embedded commercial footprint. Neither model should betreated as inherently emancipatory or inherently exploitative: both requireAfrican regulatory capacity to translate design potential into actualsovereignty gains.

The decisive variable, as thispaper has argued throughout, is not which external power "wins"Africa's digital future. Rather, it is whether African states acquire theinstitutional capacity, regulatory autonomy, and negotiating leverage needed toshape hybrid digital arrangements on their own terms. The evidence assembled inthis paper suggests that hybrid digital orders are the most probable trajectoryacross most African contexts. These hybrid systems combine Chinese connectivityinfrastructure, Indian-style interoperable protocols, Western platform layers,and domestic innovation. They are more likely than exclusive alignment witheither external model. This conclusion situates India's DPI diplomacy withinthe broader, still-unsettled debate on the future of South–South cooperation.The key question is whether emerging-power partnerships can provide genuinelydifferentiated institutional templates for the Global South. Alternatively,they may ultimately become absorbed into the same asymmetric dependencystructures that earlier waves of development cooperation sought to overcome(Mawdsley, 2012; Chaturvedi et al., 2012). The answer, on present evidence,will be determined less by New Delhi or Beijing than by the institutionalchoices African states make in the years ahead.
